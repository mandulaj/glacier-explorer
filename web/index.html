Base map

Terrain from Mapterhorn (swisstopo, BEV, IGN and others); places and borders from OpenStreetMap via OpenFreeMap. Turn the shading down to zero for a plain black-and-white map. Seabed from EMODnet Bathymetry around the Alps and coarser global terrain tiles elsewhere; the sea follows global mean sea level (Spratt & Lisiecki 2016), so past shorelines are indicative: local land movement is not included.

Since 1850

Switzerland only. Glacier outlines from the Swiss Glacier Inventories of 1850, 1931, 1973, 2010 and 2016 (GLAMOS, CC BY 4.0). Between two inventories the earlier outline fades into the later one, and the area is interpolated: glaciers did not shrink that evenly. Historic national maps (from 1844) and aerial photographs (from 1926, the latest six years of flights shown together) are served by swisstopo. The terrain is today's throughout.

Ice

Ice from a PISM simulation at 1 km resolution, Seguinot et al. 2018. Click the map for the ice history of a place, the source of its ice, or a cross-section. It is a model result, not a reconstruction: thickness is likely overestimated, and the present-day state is not today's glaciers. Erosion is a relative potential, and debris sources are assigned by nearest massif. The second ice model is the Swiss Time Machine simulation (data, built on Leger et al. 2025): finer and tuned to field evidence, but only for Switzerland and 37 to 7 ka, and published as a visualisation rather than as scientific results. Flow, erosion and debris views and the click tools keep using the whole-Alps model. Zoom out for the ice sheet over northern Europe. It is not part of the simulation: flat outlines from geological reconstructions, every thousand years from 25 to 10 ka (DATED-1, Hughes et al. 2016), coarser before, and before 38 ka one outline per climate stage (Batchelor et al. 2019), faded into each other in between.
